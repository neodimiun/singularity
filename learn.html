The date card at the top

This site uses the omega point to mean the technological singularity: the arrival of artificial superintelligence (ASI). The card shows where the best-fit curve lands if the speed-up continues, as a fitted estimate.

That single date is a fitted estimate, not a forecast and not an approved date. The same fixed method is refit every week on one list, the hand-picked models its range was calibrated on, so the card shows the same date whichever list the chart shows. How the card is built.

Below the date is the calibrated 50% range (provisional). It comes from the rolling backtest, which checks how far off this method's earlier dates were against later data. It is in-sample (measured on the same history it was fit to), so treat it as a rough guide. In 24 of the 31 held-out tests the real wait was longer than the curve said, so this method tends to name dates too early, and the range is not centred on where its errors fall. The 80% band in the tooltip is not calibrated. Neither the card nor its tooltip shows the fit's own claimed 95% range: that comes only from the curve's arithmetic and has not been shown to hold up. Each past refit's claimed 95% range appears only in the track record, as a past claim.

The line under the date gives the days to the omega point, and the same count at the previous refit, measured on that refit's own day. It says the date keeps moving away when the date moved later by at least as much time as passed between the two refits. A number marked (recomputed) is a backtest: today's method and list rerun on the data up to that day, not what the site showed then.

The main chart (Kurzweil's milestones and AI models)

The main chart plots the wait until the next event against how long ago each event happened, on log scales. Purple rings show Kurzweil's milestones (all estimates). Cyan points show the AI models from the list you pick with the switch.

The amber best-fit curve (a quadratic on the log-log chart) runs through Kurzweil's milestones and the picks toward the card's date. Zoom in past today and it turns dashed, carrying the same curve forward to ask what happens if the speed-up continues. It runs down to the omega point, where the fitted wait runs out, inside a shaded band showing the 50% range (provisional). It is a fitted estimate, not a forecast, refit every week.

The approved verdict, word for word: “artifact of the chart's construction; fails the held-out test; AI-only fit gives no near-term date” (status of curated_release_lists, approved in #25). Series status.

About the lists: On the hand-picked lists, people chose which models count. The lists hold only names, dates and gaps, with nothing fitted. Switching lists shows how much the picture depends on which models count. The lists on the switch: Old chart's 11 picks; 41 picks.

The McKenna panel

A formula, not data. It draws Terence McKenna's timewave on its own fixed scale; nothing on it was measured, and it says nothing about the AI series. You can end it on the card's omega point (a fitted estimate) or on McKenna's own 2012 date. Filled marks are the formula's upcoming lows; hollow marks are its past lows with the same end date, so you can hold them against what happened then. Lining lows up with events by eye is not a test: with enough events, some always line up. Pinch or scroll to zoom in and hunt for dates; the line under the chart gives the span in view, the way McKenna titled his graphs. Sources and the number set are in the McKenna panel note and Sources.

The steps chart

The steps chart draws the same waits as a staircase, showing all three lists at once: Kurzweil's milestones (estimates), every model release (by rule, from the 11 labs; nobody picks) and the hand-picked models chosen in the main chart. Each step spans from one event to the next on its list, and its height is that wait: lower steps mean shorter waits. Hollow marks are estimates or upper bounds. Buttons show or hide each list.

The drop where Kurzweil's milestones meet the AI releases comes from switching lists, not from a measured speed-up. Zoomed in past today, it shows the two registered test windows, the 50% range (provisional) and the omega point (a fitted estimate). It draws no best-fit curve: the steps are the data, and the curve is on the main chart.

The omega-point date and its track record

The card at the top of the main page shows the latest refit for one list, the reference list its 50% range was calibrated on, whatever list the chart shows: the date the fit names and its 50% range. The fit (owner's decision, #80) is a curve on the log-log chart: the log of each wait is a quadratic in the log of the time left until the omega point, through Kurzweil's milestones and the picks together, and the date is the one where that curve fits the points best (the least squared error, which is the highest R²; the time since the last release counts as a lower bound). The curve bends down to zero wait at that date. It replaced the viral chart's straight line: on the rolling held-out test (predict each next gap from the releases before it) its mean error is smaller than the straight line's, though the plain exponential, which names no date, is still better (see the held-out table below). That range is the list's last release before the refit plus the time the fit said remained, times the 50% band of the quadratic's rolling backtest held-out errors (results/predictions/part8_rd_quad.json, calibrated_range.json, #40). It is symmetric about the named date in log remaining time, so the date does not move inside it. With no range (a fit pinned to the start of its search grid, or no calibrated band) the card shows the named date alone; a fit pinned to the far edge of its grid shows no date. The range is provisional and in-sample; its tooltip also gives an 80% range, which is not calibrated. The card does not show the fit's own claimed 95% range: each past refit's claim is shown in the track-record panel as a past claim. The line under the date gives the days from the build date to the omega point, and the same count at the previous refit measured on that refit's own day; its verdict compares the two refits' own counts, and a number marked (recomputed) is a backtest, not what the site showed then. How many of the past estimates have already gone by (calendar refits whose named date is before the build date) is listed below for each list.

The card’s date was refit on the Oct 22 list of 39 picks; the list has since gained picks and is now labelled 41 picks.

Every other fitted date, the straight line's included, stays in the registry as a log of unapproved dates (R-a). passed = the named date is before today (2026-10-31, the build date). A fitted date that has passed is a failed forecast (R-b.1): drawn hollow. claimed_halfwidth_pct = half the 95% range that refit claimed (a past claim) as a percentage of the time the fit said remained (named date minus refit date); null when the fit is pinned to a grid edge or the range is unbounded above. moved_next_pct = how far the named date moved by the next refit shown here, as a percentage of the time the fit said remained (the owner's '% error'); moved_latest_pct = the same against the latest refit shown; null when either fit is pinned. ai_only = the AI-only fit at the same cut (no Kurzweil points): only whether its range is bounded and whether it is pinned to a grid edge are copied, never its date. summaries: results/predictions/recession.csv for the same list and method, over every refit in the registry, not only the ones shown (r = days the named date moved per day that passed, unpinned refits only; NA if fewer than 5).

Methods

This tracker tests whether four public series (CVE records, arXiv AI papers, 11-lab model releases and record-setting training compute) curve toward a finite-time “singularity” blow-up or follow steady exponential growth. Supporting charts show observed counts only. The main chart adds Kurzweil’s milestones (estimates), McKenna’s timewave (a formula, not data), two registered post-today CVE tests and the omega-point track record (also shown on the top date card and the calendar view): fitted estimates of where the fixed method pointed at each refit (with the 95% range each claimed then, a past claim), not a forecast.

Each series is tested against a constant-doubling exponential baseline on held-out future points, plus rolling backtests across earlier cutoffs to see whether implied dates hold still or slide with the calendar. Passing the finite-date rule requires a faster-than-exponential fit to beat all non-singular baselines (exponential and quadratic-in-log) out-of-sample, hold a bounded 95% range clear of grid edges, remain unpassed (passed dates are failed forecasts) and pass skeptic-red-team PR review. No series has passed. The omega-point track record shows fitted estimates not yet reviewed by our skeptic agent (owner decision, 2026-10-24), not a forecast. All other panels show observed counts and status labels only.

McKenna panel

McKenna's timewave (a formula, not data). Terence & Dennis McKenna's King Wen "novelty" curve (The Invisible Landscape, 1975; Kelley set, Peter Meyer's Timewave Zero). McKenna ended it on 2012-12-21. Here it ends on the omega point by default, so its lows show where the formula would put times of novelty before that date: a formula, not predicted events.

Events McKenna linked to the wave, where the panel shows them, are his own picks, chosen after the fact. Nobody registered the list in advance, so a pattern among them is not a test, and nothing here is scored. The wave’s bumps are not tested against them: that test is #20, and it has not been run.

Timewave theory: Terence McKenna and Dennis McKenna, The Invisible Landscape (1975). Number set: Kelley. Software and mathematics: Peter Meyer, fractal-timewave.com. Sampled, not exact: 3371 log-spaced samples before 1900 (2000 per decade, out to 5475 years before the anchor), daily values at 00:00 UTC from 1900-01-01, then 1986 log-spaced samples within 64 days before the anchor (400 per decade), generated by scripts/timewave_export.py. Exact evaluation at every zoom is #16. No claim that dips line up with events: that is McKenna's claim, and it has not been tested here.
